How to read this atlas

Every module presents two parallel frameworks. Traditional naturopathic reasoning captures the practitioner's internal logic, while the modern biochemical parallel examines the exact same mechanism through human physiology and clinical evidence.

Traditional: "Mucus and acid clog the body; clear them and the body self-heals."
Modern: "Diet changes urinary pH and net acid load, while blood pH stays tightly buffered at 7.35–7.45."

Toggle practitioner pills below to compare core theories, dietary rules, targeted organs, and clinical risk profiles side by side.

Terrain theory vs. germ theory

Terrain theory (traditional): Disease follows an internal environment of congestion, acidity, and mineral depletion. Microbes flourish in a compromised terrain, so the remedy is to purify the internal milieu.
Germ theory (modern): Specific pathogens cause specific infectious diseases. Host resilience (nutrition, immunity, microbiome) modulates severity, but an alkaline diet cannot prevent tuberculosis, HIV, or malaria—and most chronic conditions are non-infectious.
Clinical Synthesis: The valid kernel of terrain thinking is host resilience. Diet quality, sleep, metabolic health, and microbiome diversity measurably reduce chronic disease risk, though terrain does not replace antimicrobials or vaccines.

      Safety, Evidence & Disclaimer

      Essential clinical & historical context

      • This atlas is compiled strictly for historical, educational, and research purposes. It is not medical advice and establishes no clinician–patient relationship.
      • No diet, herbal formula, fast, or cleanse described here has been proven to cure cancer, autoimmune disease, diabetes, or neurodegenerative conditions.
      • Patients who chose alternative therapies alone for curable cancers experienced roughly 2.5 times higher mortality in a large 2018 cohort study (J Natl Cancer Inst), driven by delaying or refusing evidence-based oncology care.
      • Botanicals are pharmacologically active compounds that interact with prescription medications and can injure the liver, kidneys, or heart.
      • Dr. Sebi (Alfredo Bowman) held no medical degree. In 1987 New York charged him with practising medicine without a licence after advertising an AIDS cure; he was acquitted in 1988 on grounds that the state had not proved he made a medical diagnosis, and a subsequent civil consent agreement barred therapeutic cure claims for his products.

      Documented high-risk items in these traditions

      When to seek urgent medical care

      Seek immediate medical evaluation for chest pain, shortness of breath, confusion, fainting, persistent vomiting, severe diarrhoea, dark urine or jaundice, black or bloody stool, unexplained lumps, unintentional weight loss, symptomatic hyperglycaemia, or new neurological symptoms. Always consult your prescriber before combining herbs or fasting with insulin, sulfonylureas, anticoagulants, immunosuppressants, ACE inhibitors, diuretics, lithium, or digoxin.

      • Some support A plausible mechanism plus at least some human trial or cohort evidence for a related benefit.

      • Unsupported No credible evidence, or contradicted by it.

      About this guide

      About the Natural Healing Atlas

      Our aim

      This is a free guide for people who want to understand natural approaches to health. It lets you look up a condition or a food or herb, see what well-known natural-health teachers claimed about it, and see what science says today. Nothing is sold here: no supplements, no ads, no sponsors. An optional local profile lets you save personalised plans on your own device.

      Who the statements come from

      The guide does not invent new treatments. Traditional statements are credited by name to the practitioner who taught them: Dr. Sebi, Arnold Ehret, Dr. Robert Morse, Dr. Max Gerson, Barbara O’Neill and Dr. Bernard Jensen. “Modern” statements summarise mainstream clinical and physiological literature and are labelled as such. The two are kept apart so you can always tell a belief from a finding.

      How evidence is graded

      Each entry and doctrine carries one of four grades: some support, limited, unsupported, or safety concern. The grade judges the specific health claim, not whether a food is nutritious. The method is explained on the Sources page.

      Editorial standards

      • No doses and no treatment schedules. Dosing needs a clinician who knows you.
      • Every condition page lists red-flag symptoms and the medicines it can clash with.
      • Statements about real people (credentials, court and regulatory history) cite a record and are corrected when a better source appears.
      • Journal citations are listed for checking; verify any you plan to rely on in PubMed.
      • This guide has not yet been reviewed by an independent clinical panel. Treat it as a well-organised starting point, and ask your own clinician about anything you act on.
